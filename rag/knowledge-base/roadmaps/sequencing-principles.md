# How to sequence learning

The order you learn things in matters more than which resources you use. A good
resource studied too early is wasted; a mediocre one studied at the right moment
works fine.

## Prerequisites are not suggestions

If a skill depends on another, studying it early does not produce partial
understanding — it usually produces memorised procedure with no model
underneath. The classic example is React before JavaScript: the result is
someone who can assemble components but cannot debug them, because every error
message is about JavaScript.

This is why SkillForge orders roadmaps by prerequisite depth rather than by
importance. A skill you urgently need but cannot yet understand is not a good
next step, however high it scores.

## Breadth-first at each layer

Skills at the same prerequisite depth can be learned in parallel, and there is
usually an advantage to doing so. Related concepts learned around the same time
reinforce each other, and switching between them provides the spacing that makes
retention work.

Going depth-first — mastering one branch completely before touching another —
tends to produce narrow capability and a long stretch before anything useful can
be built.

## Build something at every layer

A skill that has never been used in a project decays quickly and was probably
never held properly to begin with. The reliable test of whether you know
something is whether you can use it without the tutorial open.

Projects also surface gaps that study does not. Reading about database indexes
teaches you what they are; watching your own query take four seconds teaches you
why they matter.

## Retention is a separate problem from learning

Learning something once is not the same as still knowing it in three months.
Without deliberate review, most of what you study decays on a predictable curve
— fast at first, then levelling off.

Spaced repetition addresses this directly: reviewing at increasing intervals,
timed to just before you would have forgotten. This is why SkillForge schedules
re-assessment per skill rather than treating a passed assessment as permanent.
A skill marked "mastered" six months ago and never revisited is not a reliable
claim.

## Signs your sequencing is wrong

**Everything feels like memorisation.** You are missing a prerequisite. The
concepts have nothing to attach to.

**You can follow tutorials but freeze on a blank file.** You have been consuming
rather than building. Drop a layer and build something small.

**You keep relearning the same thing.** You are not reviewing. This is a
retention problem, not a comprehension problem, and more study will not fix it.

**Progress feels fast but shallow.** Common when skipping fundamentals in favour
of frameworks. It works until the first unfamiliar error.
